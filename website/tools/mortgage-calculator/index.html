How Mortgage Payments Work

A monthly mortgage payment can include four main components, commonly called PITI: principal, interest, property taxes and homeowners insurance. Depending on your loan and property, you may also pay private mortgage insurance (PMI) and homeowners association (HOA) fees.

  • Principal: The portion of your payment that reduces the outstanding loan balance.
  • Interest: The cost of borrowing money from your lender.
  • Property taxes: Taxes assessed by local government.
  • Homeowners insurance: Coverage for eligible property-related losses.
  • PMI and HOA: Additional expenses that may apply depending on your mortgage and property.

Understanding each component helps you estimate the full monthly cost of owning a home rather than focusing only on the loan repayment.

The trade-off is simple: a shorter term costs more each month but far less interest overall, while a longer term lowers the monthly payment and raises total interest. Choose the term you can sustain, not just the one that looks cheapest in a single month.

How Mortgage Amortization Works

Mortgage amortization is the process of repaying a loan through scheduled payments over time. With a typical fixed-rate mortgage, early payments allocate a larger portion toward interest. As the outstanding balance decreases, more of each payment goes toward principal.

Your amortization schedule shows how much interest and principal you pay during each period, your remaining loan balance and when the loan is expected to be paid off.

Use the schedule above to see how your mortgage balance changes throughout the loan term and how much interest accumulates over time.

Understanding Your Mortgage Costs

Three important factors influence your mortgage payment: down payment, interest rate and loan term.

Down payment

Your down payment is the amount you contribute toward the home's purchase price before financing the remaining balance. A larger down payment reduces the amount you borrow and may help you avoid PMI on eligible conventional loans. However, using all your savings upfront can leave less money for closing costs, maintenance and emergencies.

Interest rate

Your mortgage interest rate determines how much you pay to borrow money. A higher interest rate generally increases your monthly principal-and-interest payment and the total interest paid over the loan term. Compare different rates to understand how even a small change can affect your borrowing costs.

Loan term

The loan term determines how long you have to repay your mortgage. A 15-year mortgage generally has higher monthly payments but lower total interest than a comparable 30-year mortgage. A longer term spreads repayment over more months, reducing the required monthly payment while increasing the total borrowing cost.

How Extra Mortgage Payments Save Interest

Additional payments applied directly to your mortgage principal reduce the outstanding balance. Since future interest is calculated using the remaining balance, paying extra can reduce total interest and shorten your repayment period.

You can test additional monthly or one-time payments to estimate potential savings and an earlier payoff date.

For example, adding $200 per month to the default loan in this calculator is projected to pay it off in 281 months instead of 360, saving approximately $105,428.67 in interest. This assumes the additional payments are applied directly to principal and the other loan assumptions remain unchanged.

Confirm with your mortgage servicer how extra payments are applied. Some lenders require an explicit instruction to apply additional funds toward principal.

Choosing the Right Mortgage

Mortgage programs differ in their interest-rate structures, eligibility requirements and insurance costs.

  • Fixed-rate mortgage: Keeps the interest rate unchanged throughout the agreed loan term, providing predictable principal-and-interest payments.
  • FHA mortgage: A government-insured loan with specific eligibility, down payment and mortgage insurance requirements.
  • Adjustable-rate mortgage (ARM): Starts with an initial fixed-rate period, after which the interest rate can change according to the loan agreement.

Compare the payment structure, mortgage insurance, eligibility requirements and potential long-term costs before choosing a loan. A lower initial payment does not necessarily mean a lower overall borrowing cost.

Understanding Private Mortgage Insurance (PMI)

Private mortgage insurance is commonly required on conventional mortgages when the down payment is below 20%, although requirements vary by loan and lender.

This calculator uses a simplified estimate based on your entered annual PMI rate. It calculates PMI on the loan amount and stops the estimated charge when the remaining balance reaches 80% of the home's purchase price.

Actual PMI cancellation depends on your mortgage agreement and applicable rules. For many conventional loans with borrower-paid PMI, you may request cancellation at 80% of the home's original value, subject to conditions. Automatic termination generally applies at the scheduled 78% threshold when the borrower is current. FHA and other government-backed loans follow different mortgage insurance rules.

Common Mortgage Mistakes to Avoid

  • Ignoring additional housing costs: Principal and interest are not the only expenses. Include property taxes, homeowners insurance, PMI and HOA fees when estimating affordability.
  • Choosing a loan based only on the monthly payment: A lower payment over a longer term can result in substantially more interest over the life of the mortgage.
  • Overlooking the down payment's impact: Your down payment affects the amount borrowed, monthly payment and potential mortgage insurance requirements.
  • Assuming extra payments automatically reduce interest: Additional payments need to be applied to principal to reduce the outstanding balance and future interest.
  • Relying on a single interest-rate assumption: Compare different rates to understand whether your budget can handle changes in borrowing costs.

Frequently Asked Questions About Mortgages

How Much Down Payment Do You Need to Buy a House?

The required down payment depends on the mortgage program and your eligibility. Some conventional loans allow down payments below 20%, while eligible FHA borrowers may qualify with 3.5% down. VA and USDA loans also offer qualifying borrowers options with no down payment. Remember to budget for closing costs and other upfront expenses.

How Does Your Credit Score Affect a Mortgage?

Your credit score can influence mortgage eligibility, interest rates and loan terms. A stronger credit profile may help you qualify for more competitive financing, while a lower score can limit available options or increase borrowing costs. Lenders also consider income, existing debt, savings and other financial information.

What Is Included in a Monthly Mortgage Payment?

A mortgage payment may include principal, interest, property taxes and homeowners insurance. These components are commonly called PITI. Depending on your mortgage and property, you may also pay PMI, HOA fees and other housing expenses.

When Can PMI Be Removed From a Mortgage?

For many conventional loans with borrower-paid PMI, you can request cancellation when your scheduled loan balance reaches 80% of the home's original value, subject to applicable conditions. Automatic termination generally occurs at 78% of the original value when payments are current. Other mortgage programs have different insurance rules.

Is a 15-Year Mortgage Better Than a 30-Year Mortgage?

A 15-year mortgage generally has higher monthly payments but lower total interest than a comparable 30-year loan. A 30-year mortgage spreads payments over a longer period, making monthly payments more manageable but increasing total borrowing costs. The better option depends on your budget and financial priorities.

How Can You Reduce Your Total Mortgage Interest?

You can potentially reduce total mortgage interest by making a larger down payment, choosing a shorter loan term, securing a lower interest rate or making additional principal payments. Compare these options carefully and consider how much cash you need to keep available for other expenses.
