How the mortgage payment is calculated

Principal and interest use the standard fixed-rate formula: M = P × r(1 + r)n ÷ ((1 + r)n − 1), where P is the loan amount, r is the monthly interest rate (annual rate ÷ 12), and n is the number of monthly payments. At 0% interest, the payment is simply P ÷ n. Property tax and home insurance are each divided by 12, HOA dues are added monthly, and PMI is added only while it applies. Extra monthly and one-time payments go straight to principal, which shortens the loan and reduces total interest.

FHA loans. Selecting FHA adds the 1.75% upfront mortgage insurance premium (MIP) to the loan amount and charges annual MIP monthly: 0.50%–0.55% of the loan per year on base loans up to $726,200 (0.70%–0.75% above that), lasting 11 years when the loan-to-value ratio is 90% or less and the life of the loan above that, per HUD Mortgagee Letter 2023-05. FHA loans require at least 3.5% down with a credit score of 580 or higher.

Adjustable-rate loans. For a 5-year or 7-year ARM, the rate holds for the fixed period, then adjusts once a year toward the assumed rate you set — moving at most 2% at the first adjustment and 1% at later ones, never above your starting rate plus 5% and never below 1%. The payment is recalculated at each adjustment on the remaining balance, and the schedule, chart and totals all use that projection.

Location. Choosing a state fills the rate, property tax and home insurance fields with that state's published averages (see Sources). Every figure stays editable, and if you change the home price after choosing a state, the tax updates with it until you type your own tax figure.

The trade-off is simple: a shorter term costs more each month but far less interest overall, while a longer term lowers the monthly payment and raises total interest. Choose the term you can sustain, not just the one that looks cheapest in a single month.

Extra payments

Extra principal reduces the balance that future interest is charged on, so the loan can finish early. On the default loan, adding $200 a month with no other change finishes the loan in 281 months instead of 360, saves $105,428.67 in interest, and finishes 6 years 7 months sooner. Check with your servicer how extra payments are applied — some need an explicit “apply to principal” instruction.

PMI

This calculator uses a simplified PMI rule: if the down payment is below 20%, it charges PMI at your entered annual rate on the loan amount, divided by 12, and stops once the balance is no longer above 80% of the home price. For example, a $400,000 home with 10% down gives a $360,000 loan, $2,275.44 principal and interest, $150.00 PMI a month, and a $2,975.44 total with the default tax and insurance; PMI runs for about 95 months on those inputs. That is an estimate of the simplified rule, not a promise about a real loan. For many U.S. conventional loans with borrower-paid PMI, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) describes a different set of rights: you may request cancellation in writing at a scheduled 80% of the home's original value, subject to being current and other conditions, and the servicer must generally terminate PMI automatically at a scheduled 78% if you are current. FHA and VA loans and lender-paid mortgage insurance follow different rules.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing loans by the interest rate alone. The same rate on different terms, taxes or insurance produces very different totals. Compare the full monthly payment and the total interest over the time you realistically expect to keep the loan.
  • Quoting the principal-and-interest figure as "the payment". Property tax, homeowners insurance, HOA dues and PMI are real monthly costs. A quote that leaves them out is not comparable with one that includes them.
  • Assuming PMI lasts the whole loan. PMI is normally removable once the balance reaches 80% of the home’s value, and terminates automatically at 78% on most loans — the PMI section above explains both routes.
  • Testing only today’s rate. Run the same home at a rate half a point higher and lower. If the higher payment would strain the budget, the home — not the rate — is the risk.

Frequently Asked Questions About Mortgages

How Can a Mortgage Calculator Help You?

A mortgage calculator helps you understand the financial commitment involved in purchasing a home. Instead of looking only at the property's asking price, you can estimate the monthly cost of borrowing and see how different loan details affect your budget. Try changing the down payment, interest rate, or repayment period. Comparing these figures can help you understand which loan arrangement may be more manageable before speaking with a lender.

What Is a Mortgage?

A mortgage is a loan used to purchase or refinance real estate. A lender provides the funds, and the borrower agrees to repay the loan with interest over a specified period. The property acts as security for the loan. Mortgage payments are usually made monthly. If the borrower does not meet the loan obligations, the lender may have legal rights to begin foreclosure proceedings.

How Are Mortgage Payments Calculated?

A typical mortgage payment includes four main parts, commonly called PITI: principal reduces the outstanding loan balance; interest is the cost charged by the lender for borrowing money; property taxes are assessed by local authorities; and homeowners insurance helps protect the home against covered damage and other insured risks. Depending on your loan and property, the total housing expense may also include private mortgage insurance (PMI), homeowners association (HOA) fees, or other costs. These expenses are not always collected as part of the lender's monthly payment.

How Much Down Payment Do You Need?

Your down payment is the amount you contribute toward the home's purchase price before financing the remaining balance. A larger upfront contribution generally means borrowing less, which can reduce your monthly principal-and-interest payment. A 20% down payment may help borrowers avoid PMI on many conventional loans, but it is not a universal requirement. Some eligible buyers may qualify for FHA, VA, or USDA financing with different down payment rules. Remember to reserve money for closing costs and other expenses rather than using all your savings for the down payment.

How Do Mortgage Lenders Decide How Much You Can Afford?

Lenders assess several parts of your financial situation before deciding whether to approve a mortgage and how much you may borrow. Income helps lenders assess whether you can manage the proposed monthly payments. Existing debt from credit cards, vehicle loans, student loans, and other recurring obligations affects how much money remains available for housing. Debt-to-income ratio (DTI) compares monthly debt payments with gross monthly income. Lenders use it alongside other financial information, and acceptable limits depend on the loan program and borrower profile. Your credit history and savings can also influence loan eligibility, interest rates, and terms.

What Are the Main Types of Mortgages?

Mortgage programs offer different repayment structures and eligibility requirements. A fixed-rate mortgage keeps the interest rate unchanged throughout the loan term. An adjustable-rate mortgage (ARM) may change after an initial period. FHA financing is government-insured and has specific down payment and mortgage insurance requirements. VA mortgages serve qualifying veterans, service members, and certain surviving spouses. USDA mortgages serve eligible properties and borrowers who meet location and income requirements. Compare eligibility rules, fees, insurance costs, and long-term expenses before selecting a mortgage.

How Does a 15-Year Mortgage Compare With a 30-Year Mortgage?

A 15-year mortgage has fewer scheduled payments, so borrowers generally pay less total interest when comparing otherwise similar loans. However, the monthly payment is usually higher. A 30-year mortgage spreads repayment over a longer period, which can make the required monthly payment lower, but the borrower may pay substantially more interest over the full term. Use the calculator to compare both options using the same loan amount and interest rate.

How Can You Lower Your Mortgage Payments?

Compare offers from multiple lenders to find competitive rates and fees. Consider a larger down payment if it does not leave you without adequate savings. Choose a home with a purchase price that fits your monthly budget. Compare shorter and longer loan terms. Evaluate discount points carefully, since paying upfront may reduce your rate but may take time to recover through savings. Review refinancing options if market rates or your financial circumstances change.

How Can Mortgage Pre-Approval Help You Buy a Home?

Mortgage pre-approval gives you an initial assessment of how much a lender may be willing to finance after reviewing your financial information. It can help you establish a realistic property budget and demonstrate to sellers that you have taken steps toward arranging financing. Pre-approval differs from pre-qualification, which may rely on information you provide without the same level of document verification. Neither guarantees final approval. Lenders still need to complete underwriting and review the property.

How Long Does Mortgage Pre-Approval Last?

A pre-approval letter usually has an expiration date set by the lender. Its validity period varies, so check the letter rather than assuming it remains active for a fixed number of months. If it expires before you find a property, the lender may ask for updated income, asset, and credit information.

What Does the State Selector Change?

It fills three fields with published state averages: the interest rate (a snapshot of average 30-year fixed rates), the property tax (your home price multiplied by the state's effective tax rate from the Tax Foundation), and the home insurance premium (the NAIC's average for the state). They are starting estimates, not quotes — edit any of them, and choose "National average" to return to the sample defaults.

Common Mortgage Terms

Amortization
The process of repaying a loan through scheduled payments over time.
Biweekly payments
A payment arrangement in which half of the monthly amount is paid every two weeks. Confirm the arrangement and fees with your servicer.
Down payment
The portion of the home's price paid upfront by the buyer.
Escrow
An arrangement in which funds are held and distributed for an agreed purpose. Mortgage escrow accounts may be used to pay taxes and insurance.
Extra payment
An additional amount paid toward a mortgage, often to reduce principal.
Home sale proceeds
The money remaining from a property sale after paying the mortgage balance, liens, and applicable selling expenses.
Loan amount
The principal borrowed from a lender.
Loan-to-value ratio (LTV)
The loan amount expressed as a percentage of the property's value or purchase price, depending on the lender's calculation.
Mortgage rate
The interest rate charged on the borrowed amount.
Mortgage refinance
Replacing an existing mortgage with a new loan, potentially to change the rate, term, or payment structure.
Mortgage term
The scheduled period for repaying the loan, such as 15 or 30 years.
Lump-sum payment
A one-time additional payment made toward the mortgage balance.
Gross income
Income before taxes and other payroll deductions.

Important Calculator Information

This calculator provides estimates for planning purposes and does not represent a mortgage offer, approval, or lender quote. Actual payments and borrowing costs depend on your loan agreement, property taxes, insurance, mortgage insurance rules, fees, and other circumstances. Confirm all figures with your lender before making a financial decision.
