How the return is built

Total profit has three parts: all the cash flow collected over the hold, the appreciation gain (sale value minus purchase price), and the loan paydown (the principal you repaid, which becomes equity) — minus the selling costs at exit, with the whole thing measured against the cash you actually put in (down payment plus closing costs). Total ROI is that profit divided by cash invested; annualized ROI is the geometric yearly rate that compounds to the same total over the hold, so a 10-year hold and a 5-year hold can be compared fairly. The year-by-year table shows how the equity and cash flow build up — slowly at first, faster later, because the mortgage payment stays fixed while rents grow.

What moves the answer

Leverage dominates: a 20% down payment means every dollar of appreciation or paydown is earned on five dollars of property, which is why total ROI runs far above cash-on-cash. After that, the hold length matters most — appreciation compounds, and the later years contribute disproportionately. Selling costs are the silent reducer: 6% of a grown value is a much bigger number than 6% of the price you paid, so a short hold can lose money even on a property that appreciated.

Important note

This is an informational estimate, not investment advice. It ignores taxes, vacancies beyond the allowance, major repairs, refinancing, and rent or expense shocks — and past appreciation is not a promise about the next ten years. Verify rents, costs and growth assumptions with local records before making any buying decision.

ROI real estate calculator: quick answer

Quick answer: An ROI real estate calculator projects the total return over a whole hold period, not just one year: cash flow, appreciation, loan paydown and sale proceeds, against the cash invested. On this page’s defaults, a 10-year hold gives $139,086.80 total profit, 201.58% total ROI and 11.67% annualized ROI.

Cash-on-cash return tells you one year. A hold is longer than that, and its return comes from three different places at once. Separating cash flow, appreciation and paydown shows which of the three is actually doing the work, and which is just assumed growth.

Worked example

With the values already filled in — a $300,000 price, $60,000 down plus $9,000 closing costs, 7% over 30 years, $2,500 rent, 5% vacancy, $650 monthly expenses, 3% appreciation, a 10-year hold, 6% selling costs and 2% rent and expense growth — cash invested is $69,000, total cash flow over the hold is $35,052.11, value at sale is $403,174.91, loan balance is $205,949.72, equity is $197,225.19, net sale proceeds are $173,034.70, appreciation is $103,174.91 and loan paydown is $34,050.28. Total profit is $139,086.80, total ROI is 201.58% and annualized ROI is 11.67%. Taxes are not included.

Frequently asked questions

What is ROI in real estate?

Return on investment over a hold period: total profit divided by the cash you actually invested. Here profit includes cash flow, appreciation and loan paydown, minus selling costs, and cash invested is down payment plus closing costs.

How is annualized ROI different from total ROI?

Total ROI is the whole return over the hold. Annualized ROI is the steady yearly rate that would compound to that same total over the same number of years, so holds of different lengths can be compared.

Why is total ROI much higher than cash-on-cash return?

Because leverage multiplies appreciation and paydown, and because total ROI counts the whole hold, not one year. A 20% down payment means the property is five times the cash invested.

What happens if appreciation is zero?

The deal still earns cash flow and paydown, but far less. On these defaults with 0% appreciation, total profit falls to $42,102.39, total ROI to 61.02% and annualized ROI to 4.88%.

Are taxes included?

No. Taxes are not included in this calculator. Run the exit gain through the capital gains calculator for the after-tax picture.

Is past appreciation a promise about the next ten years?

No. This is an informational estimate, not investment advice. It ignores taxes, vacancies beyond the allowance, major repairs, refinancing and rent or expense shocks.

Limitations and next steps

Verify rents, costs and growth assumptions with local records before making any buying decision. Past appreciation is not a promise about the next ten years.
