Understanding the numbers

Adjusted basis is what the property cost you for tax purposes: purchase price, plus capital improvements and buying costs, minus depreciation claimed. Amount realized (sale price minus selling costs) minus adjusted basis is the capital gain. Every documented dollar of improvements or buying costs raises basis and lowers the gain.

Depreciation recapture is the part of the gain caused by depreciation you claimed earlier. That slice is never covered by the primary-residence exclusion. It is taxed first, at your ordinary rate capped at 25%, before the rest of the gain is taxed. Held a year or less, the whole gain is taxed at ordinary rates instead.

The long-term capital gains rate applies to the remaining gain when you owned the property more than a year. Enter 0%, 15% or 20% here; which one applies depends on your income and filing status, which this calculator does not compute from your income.

The primary-residence exclusion can remove up to $250,000 of gain, or $500,000 filing jointly, but only for a primary residence meeting the 2-of-5-year ownership and use test and held more than a year. It applies only to the gain left after recapture, and no prior exclusion in the last 2 years is assumed.

The 3.8% net investment income tax (NIIT) is an extra tax that can apply to the taxable gain for higher earners. Here it is charged on the full taxable gain only when you say it applies; the calculator does not check your income against a threshold, so only turn it on if it applies to you.

How the tax works

First the gain: sale price minus selling costs gives the amount realized; purchase price plus improvements plus buying costs minus depreciation gives the adjusted basis; the difference is the capital gain. For a primary residence that meets the 2-of-5-year ownership and use test, up to $250,000 of the gain ($500,000 married filing jointly) is excluded — but the part caused by depreciation is never excludable; it is recaptured first and taxed at your ordinary rate, capped at 25%. What remains is taxed at long-term rates (0%, 15% or 20%) if you owned the property more than a year, or at ordinary rates if you owned it a year or less. High earners may also owe the 3.8% net investment income tax on the taxable gain, and most states tax it too.

What moves the answer

Three things matter more than people expect: depreciation claimed in earlier years (it lowers your basis, so it raises the gain, and then gets taxed again as recapture), the exclusion (it can erase up to $500,000 of gain on a qualifying home sale, which is why the 2-year test is worth planning around), and the holding period (crossing the one-year mark can cut the rate on the whole gain from your ordinary rate to 15%). Selling costs and improvements are the quiet reducers — every documented dollar there is a dollar less gain.

Capital gains tax on real estate calculator: quick answer

Quick answer: A capital gains tax on real estate calculator estimates the tax on a property sale: the gain, any primary-residence exclusion, depreciation recapture, and net proceeds after tax. On this page’s investment example, a $650,000 sale gives a $233,000 gain, $59,654 total tax, and $551,346 net proceeds after tax.

The tax on a sale is rarely just gain multiplied by one rate. Selling costs and basis change the gain, depreciation already claimed changes it again and is taxed as recapture, and exclusion, long-term rates, state tax and the net investment income tax can all apply to different parts of the same sale. This calculator separates those parts so each can be checked.

Worked example

With the values already filled in — a $650,000 sale, a $400,000 purchase price, $30,000 improvements, $8,000 buying costs, 6% selling costs, $60,000 depreciation claimed, an investment property held more than a year, single filing, a 15% long-term rate, a 22% ordinary rate, a 5% state rate and net investment income tax applying — the amount realized is $611,000, the adjusted basis is $378,000, and the gain is $233,000. Because this is an investment property with no years lived in as a main home, no exclusion applies. Depreciation recapture tax is $13,200, long-term gains tax is $25,950, federal tax is $39,150, net investment income tax is $8,854 and state tax is $11,650, giving total tax of $59,654 and net proceeds after tax of $551,346, an effective rate of 25.60% on the gain.

Key takeaways

  • Gain equals amount realized (sale price minus selling costs) minus adjusted basis (purchase price plus improvements plus buying costs minus depreciation).
  • Depreciation lowers basis, so it raises the gain, and then it is taxed again as recapture at the ordinary rate, capped at 25%.
  • For a qualifying primary residence, up to $250,000 of gain ($500,000 married filing jointly) can be excluded, subject to the 2-of-5-year ownership and use test — but the depreciation part is never excludable.
  • Crossing the one-year holding mark can cut the rate on the remaining gain from the ordinary rate to a long-term rate, which is why holding period matters so much.

Frequently asked questions

How is capital gains tax on real estate calculated?

First the gain: sale price minus selling costs, minus purchase price plus improvements plus buying costs minus depreciation. Then any exclusion, then recapture on the depreciation part, then long-term or ordinary rates on the rest, plus state tax and net investment income tax where they apply.

What is depreciation recapture?

Depreciation claimed in earlier years lowered your basis and raised the gain. That part of the gain is taxed again as recapture, at your ordinary rate capped at 25%, before the remaining gain is taxed at long-term rates.

When does the primary-residence exclusion apply?

For a primary residence that meets the 2-of-5-year ownership and use test, up to $250,000 of gain ($500,000 married filing jointly) can be excluded, with no prior exclusion in the last 2 years assumed. The depreciation part is never excludable.

What is the net investment income tax?

An additional 3.8% tax that can apply to the taxable gain for high earners. This calculator applies it only when you select that it applies to you; it does not compute your income or bracket from your income.

Why is the effective rate not equal to my long-term rate?

Because the total includes recapture at the ordinary rate, state tax and possibly net investment income tax, all on different parts of the gain. The effective rate in the example, 25.60%, is total tax divided by the gain.

Is this tax advice?

No. This is an informational estimate, not tax advice. Real returns involve your full income picture, bracket stacking, state rules, partial exclusions and nonqualified-use rules. Verify the result with a tax professional before relying on it.
