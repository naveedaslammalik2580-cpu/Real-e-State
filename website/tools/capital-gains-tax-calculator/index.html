The primary-residence exclusion

It can remove up to $250,000 of gain, or $500,000 filing jointly. This calculator applies it only when all three of these are true:

  • The property is your primary residence and you lived in it as your main home for at least 2 of the last 5 years.
  • You owned it for more than a year.
  • You haven’t used the exclusion on another home in the last 2 years (this is assumed).

The exclusion applies only to the gain left after depreciation recapture.

How the tax works

  1. Amount realized. Sale price minus selling costs.
  2. Adjusted basis. Purchase price plus improvements plus buying costs, minus depreciation claimed.
  3. Gain. Amount realized minus adjusted basis.
  4. Exclusion. For a qualifying primary residence, up to $250,000 of the gain ($500,000 married filing jointly) is excluded. The part caused by depreciation is never excludable.
  5. Recapture. The depreciation part is taxed first, at your ordinary rate capped at 25%.
  6. The rest. What remains is taxed at long-term rates (0%, 15% or 20%) if you owned the property more than a year, or at ordinary rates if you owned it a year or less.
  7. Add-ons. Higher earners may also owe the 3.8% net investment income tax on the taxable gain, and most states tax it too.

What moves the answer

Three things matter more than people expect. Depreciation claimed in earlier years lowers your basis, so it raises the gain, and is then taxed again as recapture. The exclusion can erase up to $500,000 of gain on a qualifying home sale, which is why the 2-year test is worth planning around. And the holding period matters: crossing the one-year mark can cut the rate on the remaining gain from your ordinary rate to 15%.

Selling costs and improvements are the quiet reducers. Every documented dollar there is a dollar less gain.

Capital gains tax on real estate calculator: quick answer

Quick answer: A capital gains tax on real estate calculator estimates the tax on a property sale: the gain, any primary-residence exclusion, depreciation recapture, and net proceeds after tax. On this page’s investment example, a $650,000 sale gives a $233,000 gain, $59,654 total tax, and $551,346 net proceeds after tax.

The tax on a sale is rarely just gain multiplied by one rate. Selling costs and basis change the gain, depreciation already claimed changes it again and is taxed as recapture, and the exclusion, long-term rates, state tax and the net investment income tax can all apply to different parts of the same sale. This calculator separates those parts so each can be checked.

Key takeaways

  • Gain equals amount realized (sale price minus selling costs) minus adjusted basis (purchase price plus improvements plus buying costs, minus depreciation).
  • Depreciation lowers basis, so it raises the gain, and then it is taxed again as recapture at the ordinary rate, capped at 25%.
  • For a qualifying primary residence, up to $250,000 of gain ($500,000 married filing jointly) can be excluded. The depreciation part is never excludable.
  • Crossing the one-year holding mark can cut the rate on the remaining gain from the ordinary rate to a long-term rate.

Frequently asked questions

How is capital gains tax on real estate calculated?

First the gain: sale price minus selling costs, minus purchase price plus improvements plus buying costs minus depreciation. Then any exclusion, then recapture on the depreciation part, then long-term or ordinary rates on the rest, plus state tax and net investment income tax where they apply.

What is depreciation recapture?

Depreciation claimed in earlier years lowered your basis and raised the gain. That part of the gain is taxed again as recapture, at your ordinary rate capped at 25%, before the remaining gain is taxed at long-term rates.

When does the primary-residence exclusion apply?

For a primary residence that meets the 2-of-5-year ownership and use test, up to $250,000 of gain ($500,000 married filing jointly) can be excluded, with no prior exclusion in the last 2 years assumed. The depreciation part is never excludable.

What is the net investment income tax?

An additional 3.8% tax that can apply to the taxable gain for higher earners. This calculator applies it only when you select that it applies to you; it does not work out your income or bracket.

Why is the effective rate not equal to my long-term rate?

Because the total includes recapture at the ordinary rate, state tax and possibly net investment income tax, all on different parts of the gain. The effective rate in the example, 25.60%, is total tax divided by the gain.

Is this tax advice?

No. This is an informational estimate, not tax advice. Real returns involve your full income picture, bracket stacking, state rules, partial exclusions and nonqualified-use rules. Verify the result with a tax professional before relying on it.
