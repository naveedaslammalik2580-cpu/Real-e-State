How cap rate works

Cap rate = net operating income ÷ property price × 100. It measures the property as if you bought it in cash: start with the annual rent, subtract a vacancy allowance to get effective gross income, add any other income, then subtract every operating expense — property tax, insurance, maintenance, management and HOA. What is left is the net operating income (NOI). Because financing is deliberately excluded, cap rate lets you compare two properties on the asset alone, no matter how each deal is funded.

What moves the answer

Three inputs do most of the work: the rent you can actually collect, the vacancy you assume, and the price you pay — which is why the panel shows the cap rate at 10% below and above the asking price. A lower price lifts the cap rate without the property changing at all, so a high cap rate can mean a bargain or a property with problems; always check why the rent or the price looks the way it does. The implied value runs the formula backwards: NOI ÷ your target cap rate tells you the most the property is worth to you at that return.

Important note

This is an informational estimate, not investment advice. Cap rate says nothing about your mortgage, taxes, appreciation or resale — and a single year of rent and expenses is not a guarantee of the next. Verify rents, occupancy and costs with local records before making any buying decision.

Cap rate calculator: quick answer

Quick answer: A cap rate calculator measures a rental property as if you bought it in cash: net operating income divided by price, as a percentage. On this page’s defaults, a $350,000 property with $2,200 monthly rent gives net operating income of $15,573.60 a year and a cap rate of 4.45%.

Cap rate is useful because it strips financing out of the comparison. Two properties with different loans can still be compared on the asset alone, because mortgage payments are deliberately not operating expenses and are left out.

Worked example

With the values already filled in — a $350,000 price, $2,200 monthly rent, 5% vacancy, $4,200 tax, $1,800 insurance, $1,500 maintenance and an 8% management fee — gross rent is $26,400, effective gross income is $25,080, total operating expenses are $9,506.40, and net operating income is $15,573.60 a year, or $1,297.80 a month. The cap rate is 4.45%, the gross rent multiplier is 13.26x, and the expense ratio is 37.90%. At 10% below the price the cap rate is 4.94%, and at 10% above it is 4.05%. At a target cap rate of 8%, the implied value is $194,670.00.

Key takeaways

  • Cap rate equals net operating income divided by price. Financing is excluded on purpose.
  • Rent collected, vacancy assumed, and price paid do most of the work — check why any of them looks unusual before trusting a high cap rate.
  • Price sensitivity shows how much the answer moves with price alone, without the property changing at all.
  • Implied value runs the formula backwards: net operating income divided by your target cap rate is the most the property is worth to you at that return.

Frequently asked questions

What is cap rate in real estate?

Cap rate is net operating income divided by property price, expressed as a percentage. It measures the property as if bought in cash, so financing does not affect the comparison.

How is net operating income calculated?

Start with annual rent, subtract a vacancy allowance to get effective gross income, add other income, then subtract operating expenses such as tax, insurance, maintenance, management and HOA. What remains is net operating income.

Why are mortgage payments excluded?

Because cap rate compares the asset, not the loan. A property’s income and expenses do not change just because one buyer borrows more than another. Use the mortgage calculator for the loan side.

Does a higher cap rate always mean a better property?

No. A high cap rate can mean a bargain, or a property with problems, weak rent, high vacancy or high expenses. Always check why the rent or price looks the way it does.

What does the implied value tell me?

It tells you the price at which the property would yield your target cap rate, given its net operating income. If the asking price is above that value, it does not meet your target on income alone.

Does cap rate include appreciation or taxes?

No. It says nothing about mortgage, taxes, appreciation or resale, and a single year of rent and expenses is not a guarantee of the next year.

Limitations and next steps

This is an informational estimate, not investment advice. Verify rents, occupancy and costs with local records before making any buying decision.
