Cap rate calculator: quick answer

Quick answer: A cap rate measures a rental property as if you bought it in cash: net operating income divided by property price, times 100. To get net operating income, start with annual rent, subtract a vacancy allowance to get effective gross income, add any other income, then subtract operating expenses such as property tax, insurance, maintenance, management and HOA costs. Financing is deliberately left out, so mortgage payments never enter the calculation, and two properties with different loans can still be compared on the asset alone.

Worked example

With the values already filled in — a $350,000 price, $2,200 monthly rent, 5% vacancy, $4,200 property tax, $1,800 insurance, $1,500 maintenance and repairs, an 8% management fee, no other income and no HOA costs — gross rent is $26,400, effective gross income is $25,080.00, total operating expenses are $9,506.40, and net operating income is $15,573.60 a year, or $1,297.80 a month. The cap rate is 4.45%, the gross rent multiplier is 13.26x, and the expense ratio is 37.90%. At 10% below the price the cap rate is 4.94%, and at 10% above it is 4.05%. At a target cap rate of 8%, the implied value is $194,670.00.

What moves the answer

Rent you can actually collect, the vacancy you assume, and the price you pay do most of the work. A lower price lifts the cap rate without the property changing at all, which is why the panel shows the rate at 10% below and above the asking price. A high cap rate can mean a bargain, or a property with problems, so always check why the rent or the price looks the way it does before trusting the number.

Understanding related numbers

Expense ratio is total operating expenses as a share of effective gross income; it shows how much of the income is spent running the property. Gross rent multiplier is property price divided by gross annual rent; it is a rough shortcut that ignores expenses and vacancy, so it is useful for a first comparison but never a substitute for the cap rate.

Key takeaways

  • Cap rate equals net operating income divided by price, with financing excluded on purpose.
  • Rent collected, vacancy assumed, and price paid do most of the work — check why any of them looks unusual.
  • Price sensitivity shows how much the answer moves with price alone, without the property changing.
  • Implied value runs the formula backwards: net operating income divided by your target cap rate is the most the property is worth to you at that return.

Frequently asked questions

Does a higher cap rate always mean a better property?

No. A high cap rate can mean a bargain, or a property with problems, weak rent, high vacancy or high expenses. Always check why the rent or price looks the way it does.

What does the implied value tell me?

It tells you the price at which the property would yield your target cap rate, given its net operating income. If the asking price is above that value, it does not meet your target on income alone.

How is net operating income different from cash flow?

Net operating income is income before financing, so it does not subtract mortgage payments. Cash flow is what remains after the loan is paid, which is why the two numbers answer different questions.

What vacancy should I assume?

Use what local records and comparable properties actually show, not a round number picked for convenience. An optimistic vacancy assumption flatters every other number on the page.

Can I compare cap rates across cities or property types?

Only with care. Different markets and property types carry different risks, costs and growth prospects, so a rate that looks attractive in one place may not mean the same thing in another.

Does cap rate include appreciation or taxes?

No. It says nothing about mortgage, taxes, appreciation or resale, and a single year of rent and expenses is not a guarantee of the next year.

Important note and limitations

This is an informational estimate, not investment advice. Cap rate says nothing about your mortgage, taxes, appreciation or resale, and a single year of rent and expenses is not a guarantee of the next. Verify rents, occupancy and costs with local records before making any buying decision.
