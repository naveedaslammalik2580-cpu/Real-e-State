How Much House Can I Afford?

As a starting point, keep your total monthly housing payment — principal, interest, property taxes, insurance, HOA dues and PMI — at or below 28% of your gross monthly income, and keep all of your monthly debts at or below 36%. That is the 28/36 rule: a comfort test for your budget, not a lender's approval limit.

The 28/36 rule

"How much house can I afford?" is really two questions: what will a lender let you borrow, and what payment can you live with? The 28/36 rule answers the second one. It is a long-standing guideline built on two debt-to-income ratios.

Gross monthly income
Your pay before taxes and other deductions, divided by 12. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau defines the debt-to-income ratio on this figure.
Front-end ratio
Your proposed monthly housing payment divided by gross monthly income. The housing payment is principal, interest, property tax, homeowner insurance, HOA dues and PMI.
Back-end ratio
Your housing payment plus every other monthly debt payment — car loans, student loans, minimum card payments, personal loans, child support — divided by gross monthly income.
The 28/36 rule
Keep the front-end ratio at or below 28% and the back-end ratio at or below 36%. Whichever cap gives the smaller housing budget binds.
See the ratio formulas

Front-end DTI = monthly housing payment ÷ gross monthly income. Back-end DTI = (monthly housing payment + other monthly debts) ÷ gross monthly income.

Guideline budget = the smaller of 28% × gross monthly income and 36% × gross monthly income − other monthly debts. That is the same logic our calculator's guideline helper uses.

The rule is a guideline, not a law and not an approval test. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau notes that different loan products and lenders set different debt-to-income limits, and it endorses no single number. A lender can approve you above these ratios — and a payment inside them can still strain your budget.

Worked example

This hypothetical example uses the calculator's guideline helper and its default loan terms. The 6.5% rate is an illustration, not a current market rate.

Worked example

From a $100,000 income to a maximum price

Inputs

$100,000 gross annual income, $500 a month in other debts, a $60,000 down payment, a 6.5% annual rate over 30 years, a 1.2% property tax rate, $1,800 annual insurance, no HOA dues, 0.5% PMI and 3% closing costs.

Calculation
  1. Gross monthly income: $100,000 ÷ 12$8,333.33
  2. Front-end cap: 28% × $8,333.33$2,333.33
  3. Back-end cap: 36% × $8,333.33 − $500$2,500.00
  4. Guideline budget: the lower of the two caps$2,333.33
  5. Maximum price at that budget$334,426.11
  6. Maximum loan: $334,426.11 − $60,000$274,426.11
  7. ResultEstimated monthly housing payment$2,333.33
  8. Cash to close: $60,000 down + 3% of the price$70,032.78
Explanation

The price is solved from the payment, not guessed: the $2,333.33 budget splits into principal and interest of $1,734.56, property tax of $334.43, insurance of $150.00 and PMI of $114.34, with no HOA dues here. The down payment is 17.9% of the price, so PMI applies; at 20% down it would not.

The front-end cap binds here because the debts are modest. With the same income but $1,500 a month in other debts, the back-end cap would be $1,500.00, and that lower budget would set the price instead. The worked example on the calculator page uses a $2,300 monthly budget with the same other terms and gives a maximum price of $330,118.43.

What lenders actually allow

The 28/36 caps are comfort targets; the limits lenders apply depend on the loan program — and they are higher.

For conventional loans, Fannie Mae's Selling Guide (B3-6-02) caps total debt-to-income at 36% for manually underwritten loans, allows up to 45% with the credit score and reserves its Eligibility Matrix requires, and allows 50% for loans approved through its automated Desktop Underwriter system. For FHA loans, HUD Handbook 4000.1 sets 31% housing and 43% total as the standard limits, rising to 40% and 50% with two compensating factors.

The 43% figure you may see quoted as a ceiling comes from the old Qualified Mortgage rule. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau replaced that hard debt-to-income cap with a price-based test in 2021, so a loan can now qualify as a Qualified Mortgage above 43% debt-to-income. Individual lenders may still apply stricter limits than the programs allow.

Approval is not affordability: a lender approving a loan at 50% back-end debt-to-income is saying the loan meets its risk rules — not that the payment leaves room for savings, maintenance and the rest of your life.

What changes the result?

The same budget buys a different house when the inputs move. A higher interest rate shrinks the loan the payment supports; a larger down payment raises the maximum price and, at 20% of the price, removes PMI from the payment. Higher property tax or insurance rates leave less room for principal and interest, and every dollar of other monthly debt reduces the back-end cap. A longer term lowers the monthly payment but raises total interest.

Our mortgage guide explains how principal and interest are calculated. Taxes and insurance vary sharply by place — a 1.2% property tax rate is an illustration, not a national figure — so enter your local numbers.

Common mistakes

  • Treating the maximum as the target. The result is the highest price that fits the payment ceiling, not a recommendation. Many buyers are better served below it.
  • Using take-home pay instead of gross income. The ratios are defined on gross monthly income; budgeting on take-home pay is a stricter personal test, not the lender's ratio.
  • Counting only principal and interest. Taxes, insurance, HOA dues and PMI are part of the payment lenders count. A principal-and-interest-only figure understates the real monthly cost.
  • Forgetting the cash beyond the down payment. Closing costs, moving costs and the first months of ownership need a separate reserve — in the worked example, cash to close is $70,032.78, not $60,000.
  • Assuming today's rate. With the payment fixed, a higher rate buys a smaller loan. Test the same budget half a point higher before relying on the result.

Key takeaways

  • Start from a payment you can sustain: 28% of gross income for housing, 36% for all debts — take the lower cap.
  • The guideline budget is a comfort test, not an approval — lenders may allow 45–50% total debt-to-income.
  • Work backward from the budget to the price; taxes, insurance, HOA dues and PMI all come out of the same payment.
  • In the worked example, $100,000 of income and $500 of monthly debts give a $2,333.33 budget and a $334,426.11 maximum price.
  • A maximum price is a ceiling — leave room for closing costs, maintenance, savings and life.

Frequently asked questions

How much house can I afford on $100,000 a year?

With no other debts, the 28/36 rule gives a housing budget of $2,333 a month — 28% of $8,333 gross monthly income. At the worked example's terms, that supports a maximum price of about $334,000. Your debts, down payment, rate and local taxes move that number, so run your own figures.

What is a good debt-to-income ratio for a mortgage?

28% front-end and 36% back-end is the widely used comfort guideline for most buyers. Lenders can go higher: Fannie Mae allows up to 45% total debt-to-income on manually underwritten loans and 50% through its automated system; FHA allows 43% as standard.

Does the 28/36 rule decide whether I get approved?

No. It is a budgeting guideline, not an underwriting rule. Approval depends on the loan program's limits, your credit score, income stability, cash reserves and the lender's own rules. You can be approved above 36% back-end debt-to-income — and still find the payment uncomfortable.

Should I use gross or net income?

Gross income — pay before taxes and deductions — is what the ratios are defined on. Budgeting on take-home pay is a stricter personal test; it gives a lower, more conservative budget than the lender's ratio. A lender will still use your gross income.

What if my other debts are high?

Then the back-end cap binds: every dollar of monthly debt reduces the housing budget by a dollar under the 36% rule. Paying down a car loan or card balances is often the fastest way to raise the price you can afford.

Does this calculator pre-approve me for a mortgage?

No. The Home Affordability Calculator gives an informational estimate from the numbers you enter. It does not check your credit, employment history or loan program, and it is not a pre-approval, a lender quote or financial advice. Confirm the terms with a lender before making an offer.

How much down payment do I need?

There is no single required amount. In this calculator's model, 20% down removes PMI from the monthly payment; smaller down payments are possible with PMI added. The down payment is not the whole cash need — closing costs sit on top of it.

Affordability is a payment question before it is a price question: set the budget with the 28/36 rule, solve for the price, treat the result as a ceiling — then check the real terms with a lender.
