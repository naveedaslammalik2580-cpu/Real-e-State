Should I Rent or Buy a House?

It depends on how long you will stay and what similar homes rent for. Renting usually wins if you may move within five years, or if owning costs far more each month than renting the same home. Buying usually wins if you will stay seven years or more and the full cost of owning is close to the rent.

What the comparison really measures

The usual mistake is comparing the rent with the mortgage payment. Rent is almost entirely money you never see again; a mortgage payment is two things at once — interest you never see again, and principal that becomes equity in the home. The honest comparison is between the parts of owning you never get back and the rent, over the same number of years, including what it costs to buy and eventually to sell.

The unrecoverable costs of owning are the mortgage interest, property tax, homeowners insurance, maintenance and repairs, and the return the down payment could have earned invested elsewhere. The recoverable part is the principal you repay and any rise in the home's value. Buying also carries one-off costs: closing costs when you buy — typically 2–5% of the price, according to the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau — and roughly 6% in selling costs when you leave. Spread over three years those costs dominate the comparison; spread over fifteen, they fade.

See how the comparison works

The net cost of buying is the down payment and closing costs plus every ownership payment so far, minus what selling that year would leave after the remaining loan balance and selling costs. The net cost of renting is all rent paid so far minus the investment growth on the down payment and closing costs the renter never spent.

That is the same model our calculator uses.

The full monthly cost of owning

Take the home already filled in on the Rent vs Buy Calculator: a $350,000 home, $70,000 down (20%), a 6.5% fixed rate over 30 years. The first-year monthly cost of owning is not the mortgage payment — it is five lines:

Principal and interest on a $280,000 loan$1,769.79
Property tax (1.2% of the price)$350.00

Owning costs $761.46 a month more than renting the same home — before any equity is counted. That gap is the number to argue with, not the mortgage payment alone. The $1,769.79 principal-and-interest figure is the standard amortization payment on the $280,000 loan; you can check it on our Mortgage Calculator. PMI is $0 here because a 20% down payment puts the loan at exactly 80% of the home's value. The maintenance line is a 1%-of-price allowance, not a bill — some years it is nothing, and a new roof can take several years of it at once.

Explanation

Renting wins the seven years, but only just — and only because the stay ends before year 9. By year 10 the balance has flipped. The owner also holds $177,290.36 of equity after seven years (a $430,455.85 home value less a $253,165.49 loan balance), but that equity cost $80,500 upfront, $2,561.46 a month, and a 6% selling cost of $25,827.35 if the home were sold then.

Note what the renter's side includes: the $80,500 never spent on a down payment and closing costs is invested at 5%, growing by $32,771.58 over the seven years. A renter who spends the difference instead of investing it gives up that offset — and the breakeven moves earlier.

The breakeven year

The breakeven year is the first year in which buying's net cost drops to or below renting's. Before it, renting is cheaper on your inputs; after it, buying is — assuming the inputs hold. In the worked example it is year 9.

That is why the common rule of thumb says five to seven years: buying and selling a home costs roughly 8–11% of the price in transaction costs (2–5% to buy, about 6% to sell), and it takes several years of equity and appreciation to earn that back. But five to seven years is a typical range, not a promise — in the worked example the breakeven is year 9, and the next section moves it anywhere from year 4 to year 26. Read the breakeven off your own numbers, not off a rule — and run the calculator with pessimistic, expected and optimistic numbers before you decide.

Two quick checks: the 5% rule and the price-to-rent ratio

Before running the full calculation, two rules of thumb give a fast read on a market — and both are worth knowing, including where they go wrong.

The 5% rule. Popularized by portfolio manager Ben Felix, it estimates the annual unrecoverable cost of owning at about 5% of the home's value: roughly 1% property tax, 1% maintenance and 3% cost of capital. Multiply the price by 5% and divide by 12: for the worked-example home, $350,000 × 5% ÷ 12 = $1,458.33 a month. Rent of $1,800 is above that, so the rule says buying — but the full model says renting wins a seven-year stay. The rule ignores the $80,500 of upfront cash and the cost of selling, and its 3% cost-of-capital slice was set when rates were lower: in year one of the example, mortgage interest alone is $18,107.85, or 5.17% of the price. Use the rule as a filter; use the calculator for the decision.

The price-to-rent ratio. Divide the home price by a year's rent: $350,000 ÷ ($1,800 × 12) = 16.2. As a rough guide, under 15 tends to favor buying, 15–20 is a toss-up your timeline decides, and over 20 tends to favor renting. At 16.2 the ratio calls it a toss-up — which is exactly what the full model says for a seven-year stay. It is the fastest check, and the least precise: it says nothing about rates, taxes or how long you will stay.

See the two quick-check formulas

5% rule breakeven rent = home value × 5% ÷ 12. Price-to-rent ratio = home price ÷ (monthly rent × 12). If your rent is below the 5% rule figure, renting is likely cheaper; if it is above, buying may be.

What moves the answer

Three assumptions you cannot know in advance can each flip the result on their own: home price growth, the investment return a renter earns, and the mortgage rate. Change one at a time in the worked example and the breakeven year moves like this:

Appreciation is the biggest lever: with no price growth at all, buying takes 26 years to break even. A higher rate pushes the breakeven later, and so does a higher return on the renter's invested deposit. If buying wins only when everything goes well, you are betting on the market rather than on housing.

Renting vs buying in 2026

The national picture in late 2026 favors patience. The National Association of Realtors reported a median existing-home price of $416,700 in October 2026, up 0.4% on a year earlier — the 40th consecutive month of year-over-year increases — while the average 30-year fixed mortgage rate was 7.33% that month, according to Freddie Mac. Existing-home sales fell to a 3.75 million annual rate, the slowest since August 2010.

At that 7.33% average rate, the worked example's monthly cost of owning rises to $2,716.98 and its breakeven moves from year 9 to year 12. But national figures frame the question; local ones answer it. Prices, rents, property tax rates and rent growth vary widely by city, and a market where rent is $2,200 on a $350,000 home breaks even in year 5 while the same home at $1,500 rent takes until year 14. Check your city's numbers before you check the national ones.

Who should rent, and who should buy

The math points one way; your life points another. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau frames the choice as a lifestyle decision as much as a financial one: renting suits people with unstable employment or a likely move, while buying is a long-term commitment with maintenance costs and price swings to manage.

Renting tends to fit if you:

  • May move within about five years, for work, family or any other reason.
  • Would empty your savings on the down payment and closing costs.
  • Have unstable income, or expect it to change soon.
  • Live where comparable rents are far below the cost of owning.

Buying tends to fit if you:

  • Plan to stay at least seven years, ideally longer, in the same home.
  • Have stable income and cash left after the down payment, closing costs and an emergency fund.
  • Can absorb the full monthly cost plus surprise repairs without strain.
  • Value stability, control over the home, and a payment that does not rise with the rental market.

You do not need 20% down. FHA loans require as little as 3.5% down for borrowers with a credit score of 580 or higher, under HUD Handbook 4000.1 — $12,250 on a $350,000 home. But a smaller down payment means a larger loan, mortgage insurance, and a higher monthly cost: at 10% down the worked example costs $2,913.93 a month and breaks even in year 10. Before deciding what you can afford, work out the price your income supports — our guide How Much House Can I Afford? explains the 28/36 rule lenders use.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing rent with the mortgage payment alone. Principal and interest are $1,769.79 of the $2,561.46 monthly cost in the worked example; tax, insurance and maintenance are the rest.
  • Treating rent as money thrown away. Rent buys housing, flexibility, and freedom from tax, insurance, maintenance and price risk — and the renter's unspent down payment can grow. In the example, the $80,500 grows by $32,771.58 at 5% over seven years.
  • Assuming the home will appreciate. With no price growth the worked example takes 26 years to break even. Appreciation is a hope, not a line item.
  • Deciding on tax savings alone. Mortgage interest is deductible only if you itemize, and only on the first $750,000 of mortgage debt for loans taken out after December 15, 2017 (IRS Publication 936). Many homeowners take the standard deduction instead and get no benefit from it.
  • Buying the maximum the lender allows. Affordability and comfort are different things. The 28/36 rule caps the payment a lender will approve, not the payment you will be glad to make.
  • Assuming you will stay. A move in year three turns the transaction costs into a loss the breakeven never gets to repay.

Key takeaways

  • Compare the unrecoverable costs of owning with the rent — not the rent with the mortgage payment.
  • In the worked example, owning costs $2,561.46 a month against $1,800 rent, and renting wins a 7-year stay by $11,461.78.
  • The breakeven year is year 9: before it renting wins, after it buying wins — on these inputs.
  • The 5% rule ($1,458.33 a month) and a 16.2 price-to-rent ratio are quick filters; the full model, which includes transaction costs and your time horizon, is the decision.
  • Appreciation, the mortgage rate and the renter's investment return can each move the breakeven by years.
  • At the October 2026 average mortgage rate of 7.33%, the example's breakeven moves from year 9 to year 12.
  • Rent if you may move within five years or the down payment would empty your savings; buy if you will stay seven years or more and the full monthly cost fits comfortably.

Frequently asked questions

Should I rent or buy a house?

It depends on how long you will stay and what similar homes rent for. Rent if you may move within five years or owning costs far more each month than renting the same home. Buy if you will stay seven years or more and the full cost of owning is close to the rent.

How long do I need to stay for buying to make sense?

There is no universal number, but typically five to seven years, because buying and selling costs roughly 8–11% of the price in transaction costs. In the worked example the breakeven is year 9. Read the breakeven year off your own rent, price, rate and growth assumptions rather than relying on a rule of thumb.

What is the 5% rule for renting vs buying?

Popularized by portfolio manager Ben Felix, it estimates the annual unrecoverable cost of owning at about 5% of the home's value — roughly 1% tax, 1% maintenance and 3% cost of capital. Breakeven rent is the price × 5% ÷ 12, or $1,458.33 a month on a $350,000 home. It ignores transaction costs and was set when rates were lower.

What is a good price-to-rent ratio?

Divide the home price by a year's rent. Under 15 tends to favor buying, 15–20 is a toss-up your timeline decides, and over 20 tends to favor renting. The worked example's ratio is 16.2 — a toss-up, which matches the full model's answer for a seven-year stay.

Is renting throwing money away?

No. Rent pays for housing and flexibility, and it avoids property tax, insurance, maintenance and price risk. It also leaves the down payment free: in the worked example the renter's $80,500 grows by $32,771.58 at a 5% return over seven years. Rent is only wasted if the money saved by renting is spent instead of invested.

Do I need a 20% down payment to buy a house?

No. FHA loans require as little as 3.5% down for borrowers with a credit score of 580 or higher, under HUD Handbook 4000.1. But a smaller down payment means a larger loan, mortgage insurance and a higher monthly cost — at 10% down the worked example costs $2,913.93 a month.

Does buying a house save on taxes?

Only sometimes. Mortgage interest is deductible only if you itemize your deductions, and only on the first $750,000 of mortgage debt for loans taken out after December 15, 2017, under IRS Publication 936. Many homeowners take the standard deduction instead and get no benefit. Do not let assumed tax savings decide a rent-vs-buy choice on their own.
