What Is a Good Cap Rate for a Rental Property?

There is no single good cap rate. For rental property in 2026, 5–8% is a reasonable range in many mid-sized markets, with 4–5% common in expensive coastal cities and 8% or more in smaller, higher-risk markets. A cap rate is only good relative to similar properties in the same market — and only when the NOI behind it is honest.

What a cap rate measures

The capitalization rate, or cap rate, is the annual income yield of a property as if you paid for it entirely in cash: for every dollar the property is worth, how many cents of operating income does it produce in a year? The income is net operating income (NOI) — rent actually collected, less vacancy and operating expenses such as tax, insurance, maintenance and management, before any mortgage payment. Because financing is left out, the same property has the same cap rate whether you buy it with cash or with a mortgage — which is what makes cap rates comparable.

See the cap rate formulas

Cap rate (%) = net operating income ÷ property value × 100. Net operating income = effective gross income − operating expenses. The same relationship runs backwards to test a price: estimated value = NOI ÷ cap rate (as a decimal).

That is the same model our calculator uses.

A cap rate is a yield, not a total return. It says nothing about what the property will be worth in five years, what the mortgage will cost, or what tax you will pay. Its job is narrower: to compare the income side of properties on equal terms. The figure investors usually quote is the going-in cap rate — the rate on the property's current, first-year NOI.

Worked example

This hypothetical example uses the values already filled in on the Cap Rate Calculator, so you can check every figure there.

Worked example

A $350,000 rental at $2,200 a month

Inputs

$350,000 price, $2,200 monthly rent, 5% vacancy, $4,200 property tax, $1,800 insurance, $1,500 maintenance, an 8% management fee, no other income and no HOA costs.

Calculation
  1. Gross annual rent: $2,200 × 12$26,400.00
  2. Vacancy loss: 5% × $26,400$1,320.00
  3. Effective gross income: $26,400 − $1,320$25,080.00
  4. Management fee: 8% × $25,080$2,006.40
  5. Total operating expenses: $4,200 + $1,800 + $1,500 + $2,006.40$9,506.40
  6. Net operating income: $25,080 − $9,506.40$15,573.60
  7. ResultCap rate: $15,573.60 ÷ $350,000 × 1004.45%
  8. Gross rent multiplier: $350,000 ÷ $26,40013.26x
  9. Expense ratio: $9,506.40 ÷ $25,08037.90%
  10. Value at an 8% target cap rate: $15,573.60 ÷ 0.08$194,670.00
Explanation

The 4.45% result is modest — and that is the point. At the full $350,000 asking price this property yields less than the 5–8% many investors look for, and the same income at an 8% target implies a value of only $194,670. At 10% below the price the cap rate is 4.94%; at 10% above it, 4.05%.

Notice how much of the rent the expenses absorb: 37.90% of effective gross income goes to tax, insurance, maintenance and management. A cap rate built on gross rent instead of NOI would have looked far better — $26,400 ÷ $350,000 is 7.54% — and would have been wrong.

What counts as a good cap rate?

There is no universal number, because a cap rate prices income and risk at once. A low cap rate usually means investors expect the property to hold or grow in value; a high cap rate usually means they are being paid to take on more risk or weaker growth. What counts as good depends on the market, the property type, and what you want the property to do.

As a starting point, CBRE reported an average US multifamily cap rate of 5.8% in the first quarter of 2026. Typical ranges reported in 2026 market surveys:

Market and property typeTypical cap rateWhat it usually means
Primary gateway cities — multifamily in New York, San Francisco, Los Angeles, Boston4–5%Lowest risk, strongest appreciation expectations
Large secondary markets — Class B multifamily in Dallas, Chicago, Denver and similar cities5–7%A balance of yield and growth
Smaller and tertiary markets, single-family rentals6–9% and aboveHigher cash yield, but usually higher risk and slower appreciation

These are survey ranges, not targets for any one building, and they move with interest rates: cap rates rose across property types as rates climbed in 2022–2024, and they are expected to ease gradually as credit conditions loosen.

Compare locally, not nationally: a 6% cap rate is strong in a gateway city and ordinary in a small Midwest market. The useful comparison is recent sales of similar properties in the same area — not a national average.

What moves the cap rate

The same property can show a very different cap rate depending on the inputs.

  • Price paid. The denominator does most of the work: the same $15,573.60 of NOI yields 4.45% at $350,000 but 5.56% at $280,000. Negotiating the price is negotiating the cap rate.
  • Rent and vacancy. Higher collected rent lifts NOI; a realistic vacancy allowance lowers it. An optimistic vacancy assumption flatters every other number.
  • Operating expenses. Property tax, insurance and management vary sharply by place. A low-tax market can support a lower cap rate than a high-tax one on the same rent.
  • Interest rates. When borrowing costs rise, buyers demand higher yields, so cap rates rise; when rates ease, cap rates tend to compress.
  • Growth and risk. Markets where rents and values are expected to grow command lower cap rates; weaker demand, a declining local economy or an aging building push the rate up.

What the cap rate leaves out

A cap rate is a first filter, not a verdict. It deliberately excludes the things that decide whether a property is actually a good investment.

  • Financing. A 4.45% cap rate says nothing about your mortgage. With cheap leverage, a low-cap-rate property can still produce a strong cash-on-cash return; with expensive debt, a high-cap-rate property can still lose money monthly.
  • Appreciation and resale. A property in a growing market can return far more through value growth than its cap rate suggests — and a high-cap-rate property in a shrinking market can lose value faster than its income compensates.
  • Taxes and depreciation. Depreciation and deductible expenses change the after-tax return without touching the cap rate.
  • Capital spending and future income. A new roof or HVAC system is not an operating expense, so it never appears in NOI — yet it is a real cost. And the cap rate uses one year of rent and expenses, which rent growth, lease expirations and turnover can change quickly.

That is why a 4.45% cap rate is not automatically a bad deal and an 8% cap rate is not automatically a good one.

How to use a cap rate when comparing properties

  1. Use the same NOI definition for every property. Same vacancy allowance, same expense categories, same treatment of management. A cap rate built on different definitions is not comparable.
  2. Compare within the same market and property type. A single-family rental and a 20-unit building in different cities are not the same investment, even if their cap rates match.
  3. Run the price sensitivity. Our calculator shows the cap rate at 10% above and below the asking price — a small price change moves the yield more than most expense changes.
  4. Work backwards from your target. Divide NOI by the cap rate you want: at an 8% target, the worked example's $15,573.60 of NOI supports a price of $194,670.
  5. Sanity-check the NOI. Verify the rent against comparable listings, the vacancy against local records, and the expenses against actual tax and insurance bills. A cap rate is only as honest as its NOI.

Common mistakes

  • Using gross rent instead of NOI. Dividing the full scheduled rent by the price overstates the yield. Cap rate starts from income after vacancy and operating expenses.
  • Assuming 100% occupancy. Even a well-run rental loses some rent to empty weeks and turnover. Assuming full collection flatters every deal.
  • Putting mortgage payments into NOI. Mortgage principal and interest never enter NOI. Leaving them out is what makes cap rate comparable between a cash purchase and a financed one.
  • Chasing the highest cap rate. The highest number is often the riskiest property — a weak market, deferred maintenance, or income that will not repeat.
  • Comparing across markets or property types. A rate that looks attractive in one place may mean something entirely different in another.
  • Treating one year's NOI as permanent. A single year of rent and expenses is no guarantee of the next.

Key takeaways

  • Cap rate is NOI divided by property value — the unlevered annual yield, with financing deliberately excluded.
  • There is no single good cap rate: roughly 4–5% in gateway cities, 5–7% in large secondary markets, and 6–9% or more in smaller, higher-risk markets.
  • CBRE reported an average US multifamily cap rate of 5.8% in Q1 2026 — an anchor, not a target for any one property.
  • A higher cap rate usually prices in higher risk or slower growth, not a better property.
  • In the worked example, $15,573.60 of NOI on a $350,000 price gives a 4.45% cap rate — below the range many investors seek at full asking price.
  • Compare cap rates only between similar properties in the same market, on the same NOI definition — then judge financing, growth and taxes separately.
  • A good cap rate is a local, like-for-like comparison, not a magic number.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good cap rate for a rental property?

It depends on the market. In 2026, 5–8% is a reasonable range for many mid-sized US markets; expensive coastal cities commonly see 4–5%, while smaller markets often run 6–9% or higher. Compare it with recent sales of similar rentals nearby, not a national number.

Is a higher cap rate always better?

No. A higher cap rate often signals higher risk — a weaker neighborhood, deferred maintenance, higher vacancy or a declining local economy. A 9% cap rate in a shrinking market is not necessarily better than a 5% cap rate in a growing one.

What is the difference between cap rate and cash-on-cash return?

Cap rate is the property's unlevered yield — NOI divided by value, ignoring financing. Cash-on-cash return divides your annual cash flow after mortgage payments by the cash you invested, so it reflects your loan terms and down payment.

What is a good cap rate for a single-family rental?

Single-family rentals in 2026 commonly fall in the 5–8% range in mid-sized markets, with lower rates in expensive coastal cities and higher rates in smaller markets. Compare them with other single-family rentals nearby, not with apartment buildings.

How do you calculate a cap rate?

Divide net operating income by the property's value and multiply by 100. NOI is effective gross income — rent minus vacancy plus other income — less operating expenses. In the worked example, $15,573.60 of NOI on a $350,000 price gives 4.45%.

Does the cap rate include mortgage payments?

No. Mortgage principal and interest are financing costs, not operating expenses, and they never enter NOI. That exclusion is deliberate: it lets you compare properties without loan terms distorting the result.

Can I use a cap rate to value a property?

Yes, as a simplified income-based indication: divide NOI by the cap rate you require. At an 8% target, the worked example's $15,573.60 of NOI implies a value of $194,670. Location, condition, growth and comparable sales also set the price.
